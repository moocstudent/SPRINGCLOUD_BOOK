## The promise, and the fine print

Elasticity is the ability microservices are sold on: run three replicas for everyday traffic, grow to a dozen when a sale pours in ten times the load, then shrink back when the peak passes — paying only for the capacity you actually use. The promise is real. The fine print is that scaling is a control loop with lag, and three traps live in that lag. This chapter is about making the loop behave.

## The HPA control loop

The Horizontal Pod Autoscaler watches a metric and adjusts replica count to keep it near a target. You hand it a target — "keep average CPU at 60%" or "100 requests per second per replica" — and roughly every 15 seconds it recomputes:

    desiredReplicas = ceil( currentReplicas x currentMetric / targetMetric )

Three replicas averaging 90% CPU against a 60% target give ceil(3 x 90 / 60) = 5, so it scales to five. When the metric drops back under target, it scales down. The arithmetic is trivial; everything hard is in the timing.

```yaml
apiVersion: autoscaling/v2
kind: HorizontalPodAutoscaler
metadata:
  name: order-service
spec:
  scaleTargetRef:
    apiVersion: apps/v1
    kind: Deployment
    name: order-service
  minReplicas: 3
  maxReplicas: 20
  metrics:
    - type: Resource
      resource:
        name: cpu
        target:
          type: Utilization
          averageUtilization: 60
  behavior:
    scaleUp:
      stabilizationWindowSeconds: 0
      policies:
        - { type: Pods, value: 4, periodSeconds: 15 }
    scaleDown:
      stabilizationWindowSeconds: 300
      policies:
        - { type: Percent, value: 50, periodSeconds: 60 }
```

`minReplicas: 3` is your floor — never scale below three, so a single node loss never leaves you at one. `maxReplicas: 20` is the ceiling that protects your bill and your database from a runaway loop.

## Trap 1: cold-start lag

The HPA can decide to add a replica in one 15-second tick. That replica cannot serve for a while yet. A Spring Boot service on the JVM pays for image pull, JVM start, Spring context refresh, connection-pool warm-up and JIT compilation before its first requests run at full speed — commonly 20 to 60 seconds. Kubernetes will not route traffic to it until its readiness probe passes:

```yaml
readinessProbe:
  httpGet: { path: /actuator/health/readiness, port: 8080 }
  initialDelaySeconds: 15
  periodSeconds: 5
  failureThreshold: 3
```

During that gap the existing three replicas are still absorbing the full surge, still at 100% CPU, still timing out real requests. Scaling is not instant — the graph you want (load up, replicas up, latency flat) is a fantasy; the real graph has a latency spike in the seconds before new capacity turns green. Mitigations attack the start-up cost directly: provision headroom ahead of a known sale, keep min replicas higher, or shrink JVM start itself with CRaC (checkpoint/restore, resuming a warmed image in tens of milliseconds) or a GraalVM native image that boots in under 100 ms.

## Trap 2: flapping

Suppose traffic hovers right at the boundary that maps to 5-versus-6 replicas. Without damping, the HPA scales up, the metric drops, it scales down, the metric rises, it scales up — the replica count thrashes every tick, and every scale-up pays the cold-start cost again for nothing. The cure is a stabilisation window. `scaleDown.stabilizationWindowSeconds: 300` tells the HPA to hold a scale-down for five minutes, taking the highest recommendation seen in that window, so it releases capacity slowly and reluctantly. Scale-up is usually left fast (window 0), because being late to add capacity hurts users, while being late to remove it only costs a little money.

## Trap 3: cost versus latency headroom

The target you pick is a business decision disguised as a number. A high target packs each replica tight — cheap, but with little headroom, so a surge eats your latency before new pods arrive. A low target keeps every replica half-idle — flat latency, fat bill.

| Target CPU | Headroom | Peak replicas (same load) | P99 under a 3x surge | Relative cost |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 80% | thin | 8 | spikes to seconds | 1.0x |
| 60% | moderate | 11 | brief bump | 1.4x |
| 40% | generous | 16 | stays flat | 2.0x |

There is no correct answer, only the one your business can defend. The bench gives you an undulating traffic curve and the same three knobs — target, cold-start time, stabilisation window — so you can watch replicas chase load, timeouts appear in the scale-up gap, and the monthly bill move with the headroom you leave.

## Exercises

1. Derive the HPA formula by hand: 4 replicas at 75% CPU against a 50% target — how many replicas next tick? Then at 30% — does it scale down, and to how many?
2. Set `scaleUp.stabilizationWindowSeconds: 0` and `scaleDown` to 300, then explain in one sentence why up is fast and down is slow.
3. Measure one service's cold start: time from container start to the readiness probe passing. Is it closer to 20 s or 60 s, and what would a native image change?
4. On the bench, drop the stabilisation window to 0 and push traffic to sit on a replica boundary. Watch it flap, then restore the window and confirm it settles.
