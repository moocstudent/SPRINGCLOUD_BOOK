## Where @Transactional stops

`@Transactional` is a single database's power. Under it, Spring holds one JDBC connection, and `commit`/`rollback` are that connection's atomic operations on that one database. The moment your checkout spans an order database, an inventory database and an account database — three services, three datasources — no connection sees all three, so nothing can commit or roll them back together. If the order commits and then the account debit throws, the order row is **already durable**; Spring cannot reach into a database it does not own and undo a committed transaction. The atomicity boundary is the connection, and it does not cross the network. Everything below rebuilds all-or-nothing across that gap — and each way charges a different price.

## 2PC / XA: strong consistency, availability staked

Two-phase commit puts a **coordinator** in front of the participating resource managers. In **phase 1 (prepare)** each participant does the work, writes its undo/redo log, *holds its locks*, and answers "ready" — but does not commit. In **phase 2 (commit)** the coordinator, having collected a unanimous "ready", tells everyone to commit; a single "no" turns it into a global abort. The guarantee is genuinely strong: all commit, or none.

The price is written in the word *holds*. Every participant keeps its row locks from prepare until the phase-2 decision arrives — across two network round trips, at the speed of the slowest participant. A local transaction might hold a row lock for about 1 ms; the same row under XA is locked for tens of milliseconds, so throughput under contention collapses. Worse, if the coordinator crashes *after* prepare, participants sit **blocked**, holding locks, unable to safely commit or abort until it returns — 2PC stakes the whole group's availability on one coordinator. That blocking is why XA is rare across busy microservices.

## TCC and Saga: short locks vs a compensation chain

**TCC (Try-Confirm-Cancel)** replaces DB locks with **business reservations**. *Try* reserves — freeze 100 yuan of balance, not by locking the row across the network but by moving it to a "frozen" column and committing immediately. *Confirm* spends the reservation; *Cancel* releases it. Locks are short and local, so throughput is high, but you pay in code: three methods per operation, and Confirm/Cancel must be idempotent and cope with edge cases like *empty rollback* (Cancel arrives before Try) and *hanging* (Try arrives after Cancel).

**Saga** goes further and holds no locks at all. It is a chain of local transactions T1…Tn, each committing on its own, each paired with a **compensation** C1…Cn. If T3 fails, the saga runs C2 then C1 in reverse. The idea that trips people up: **a compensation is not a rollback.** When T2 committed it *really debited* the account; C2 cannot un-happen it, so C2 is a *new* transaction that credits the money back. Between T2 committing and C2 running there is a real interval in which an observer sees money debited and no order — an **observable inconsistency window**. Saga gives you no isolation, so you design around it with explicit states (an order sits `PENDING` until the chain completes). In return it is the only option that scales to long-running, high-throughput flows.

## Seata's four modes, and the one insight

Seata is the de-facto distributed-transaction framework in the Spring Cloud Alibaba world. A standalone **TC (Transaction Coordinator)** tracks the global transaction; your services carry the **TM** (which starts it) and the **RM** (which registers each branch).

- **AT (Automatic Transaction)** is the least-effort mode and the reason people reach for Seata. It intercepts your SQL, saves a *before-image* and *after-image* into an `undo_log` row inside the same local transaction, and takes a global lock. If the global transaction fails, Seata reads `undo_log` and generates the compensating SQL for you — **zero business code**. You pay with a global lock (real, if weaker than XA's isolation) and, being compensation-based, a small window.
- **TCC / Saga / XA modes** wrap the manual patterns above: TCC for precise resource control, Saga (a state machine) for long flows, XA for true strong consistency when you must have it.

```java
@GlobalTransactional(name = "place-order", rollbackFor = Exception.class)
public void placeOrder(OrderCmd cmd) {
    orderClient.create(cmd);       // branch 1 -> order-db
    inventoryClient.deduct(cmd);   // branch 2 -> inventory-db
    accountClient.debit(cmd);      // branch 3 -> account-db (throws -> global rollback)
}
```

| Mode | Consistency | Lock time | Business code | Coupling |
|------|-------------|-----------|---------------|----------|
| 2PC / XA | strong | long (both phases) | none | tight (all RMs XA-capable) |
| TCC | eventual (short) | short reservation | heavy (3 methods) | high |
| Saga | eventual (visible window) | none | medium (compensations) | medium |
| Seata AT | eventual (global lock) | short (until commit) | none (undo_log) | low |

The single insight to carry out: **there is no free strong consistency.** 2PC buys it with long locks and coordinator blocking; Saga buys availability with hand-written compensation and a visible window; TCC and AT sit between. Choose per business — a low-contention, money-critical debit may justify XA/AT, while a long, high-volume fulfilment flow wants Saga. On the bench, inject a failure at the third step and compare 2PC's lock-hold time against the saga's compensation chain, watching success rate, lock duration and compensation cost move against each other.

## Exercises

1. Explain in two sentences why committing the order and then calling the account service can leave money uncharged, and why swapping the order does not fix it.
2. For "deduct 100 from an account", write the Try, Confirm and Cancel steps of a TCC implementation, and state what Cancel must do if it arrives before Try.
3. Take a three-step saga (order → stock → payment) and write the compensation for each step; mark the exact window in which an outsider could see an inconsistent total.
4. On the bench, run the same failing transaction under XA and under Saga; record lock-hold time and end-to-end latency for each, and decide which you would ship for a 500-orders/second checkout.
