## One data centre is one fault domain

Put every service in one data centre and the system is simple to reason about and simple to lose: the day that building loses power, loses its uplink, or floods, the whole system is gone at once — no replica, no failover, nothing. Multi-region active-active is the answer that scares people, and rightly: you run the stack in two or three geographically separate DCs at once, serve live traffic from all of them, and when one fails wholesale, route its traffic to the survivors. Users reach the nearest healthy DC through geo-DNS or an anycast edge that resolves them to a region and shifts them off a dead one within a health-check interval.

## Replication lag: the speed of light bills you

The moment two DCs both hold data, they must copy writes to each other, and physics sets a floor on how fast. Light in fibre travels about 200,000 km/s, so 1,000 km one way is ~5 ms, a round trip ~10 ms, and real links add switching and processing to reach tens of milliseconds. That number has a user-visible consequence called read-your-writes: a user updates their address in DC A, their very next read is routed to DC B, the replica has not arrived yet, and they see the old address. Nothing is broken — the data is in flight — but it looks broken. The fixes are routing tricks: pin a user to one DC for a session (sticky routing), or route reads that follow a write back to the primary until replication catches up.

## Write conflicts: who wins when both sides write

Read lag is survivable. The hard problem is conflicting writes: true active-active lets both DCs accept writes, so when two sites modify the same record within the replication window, neither saw the other. The default resolution is last-write-wins — compare timestamps, keep the later one — which is simple and silently destroys the earlier write. Worse, clocks across DCs are not perfectly synchronised, so "later" can itself be wrong. Stricter schemes cost more: version vectors detect that two writes were concurrent rather than ordered and hand the conflict to the application; business-level merges encode the right answer per data type — two shopping carts merge by union, while an account balance must not merge at all and instead routes every write for that key to one DC. The honest design move is to make most keys single-writer and reserve real multi-writer for data that can actually merge.

## RPO and RTO: the two numbers that pick your replication

Every failover strategy is scored by two numbers. RPO (Recovery Point Objective) is the most data you can afford to lose — how far back the recovery point may sit. RTO (Recovery Time Objective) is the longest you can be down. They translate directly into a replication choice.

```ini
# Primary in DC-A -- semi-synchronous replication (my.cnf)
[mysqld]
server_id                    = 11
log_bin                      = mysql-bin
binlog_format                = ROW
gtid_mode                    = ON
enforce_gtid_consistency     = ON
rpl_semi_sync_master_enabled = 1
rpl_semi_sync_master_timeout = 1000   # ms; fall back to async after 1s
```

Synchronous replication holds the commit until a remote DC acknowledges — RPO is zero, no committed write is ever lost, but every write pays the cross-DC round trip, so a 10 ms link adds 10 ms to every commit and a far or flaky DC stalls all writes. Asynchronous replication commits locally and ships the change afterward — writes stay fast, but a DC that dies takes its un-shipped tail with it, so RPO is however many seconds of writes were in flight. The MySQL semi-sync above is the middle road: wait for one remote ack, but fall back to async after `rpl_semi_sync_master_timeout` so a slow replica cannot freeze the primary — you trade the zero-RPO guarantee for liveness exactly when a DC is struggling.

| Replication | RPO (data loss) | Write latency added | RTO on DC loss | Main risk |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Synchronous | 0 | +1 cross-DC RTT per write | low | a slow/far DC stalls every commit |
| Semi-sync (1 ack, timeout) | ~0, seconds on fallback | +1 RTT, or 0 after timeout | low–med | fallback silently drops the guarantee |
| Asynchronous | seconds of writes | none (local commit) | med | promote loses the un-replicated tail |

Redis and caches usually replicate async for speed and treat the cache as rebuildable; the system of record is where you spend on stronger guarantees. The bench simulates two DCs so you can dial replication lag, switch read and write modes, and inject a DC-level failure — watching a read miss its own write, a conflict resolve by last-write-wins, and RPO/RTO move as you change the strategy.

## Exercises

1. Given a 1,500 km link, estimate the minimum one-way replication lag from the speed of light in fibre, then explain why the measured lag is larger.
2. Two DCs increment the same counter from 10, to 11 and to 12, within the replication window. Show what last-write-wins yields and why it is wrong; propose a merge that is not.
3. Your business allows losing at most 5 seconds of orders (RPO = 5 s) but needs sub-10 ms writes. Which replication mode fits, and what is the residual risk?
4. On the bench, set replication lag to 200 ms and issue a write then an immediate cross-DC read. Reproduce the stale read, then enable sticky routing and confirm it disappears.
