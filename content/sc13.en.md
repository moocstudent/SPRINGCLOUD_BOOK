## The hidden cost of a synchronous call

When the order service calls inventory, points and notification with three blocking HTTP calls, it looks like ordinary code, but you have quietly bought **temporal coupling**: all four services must be healthy *at the same instant*, and the caller holds a thread until the slowest of them answers. The bill lands on two axes. Latency **adds up** — if each downstream has a p99 of 200 ms, a serial chain is 600 ms before the user sees anything, and the order thread is pinned that whole time. Availability **multiplies down** — three dependencies at 99.9% each give the checkout at most 0.999 × 0.999 × 0.999 ≈ 99.7%, and every downstream you bolt on makes it worse. Worst of all, if the notification service — the least important of the three — is down, a naive synchronous checkout **fails the whole order** over something the user never asked about.

## Publish once, whoever cares subscribes

Event-driven design breaks the coupling by inverting who waits. The order service commits its own row and publishes one fact — `OrderCreated` — then returns. It does not know or care who consumes it. Inventory decrements stock, points accrues rewards, notification sends an email, each on its own, none aware of the others. Three properties fall out:

- **Decoupling.** Adding a fourth reaction — fraud scoring, say — is a new subscriber; the order service does not change, redeploy, or even know.
- **Peak-shaving.** A flash sale pushing 10,000 orders/second at a database that sustains 2,000 writes/second would topple a synchronous chain. As events, the surplus simply queues; consumers drain at their own steady rate, trading latency for survival.
- **Resilience.** If the inventory consumer crashes, its messages **wait in the queue** for its return and are processed on recovery. The synchronous version has no such grace: a call to a dead service fails immediately, on the user's request.

The trade you accept in return is that the world becomes **asynchronous and eventually consistent** — the order exists a few hundred milliseconds before stock is actually decremented, and you must design for that gap (chapters sc14–sc15).

## Spring Cloud Stream's functional model

Spring Cloud Stream lets you write this without touching a Kafka or RabbitMQ client directly. You express each role as a plain `java.util.function` bean:

- `Supplier<T>` — a source that only emits (the producer),
- `Function<T,R>` — consume-and-produce (a processor),
- `Consumer<T>` — a sink that only receives.

A **binder** connects those beans to real infrastructure; swapping `spring-cloud-stream-binder-kafka` for `...-binder-rabbit` moves the same code from Kafka to RabbitMQ with no source change.

```java
@Bean
public Consumer<OrderCreated> orderEvents() {
    return event -> inventory.decrement(event.sku(), event.qty());
}
```

```yaml
spring:
  cloud:
    function:
      definition: orderEvents
    stream:
      bindings:
        orderEvents-in-0:          # <bean>-in-0 is the input channel
          destination: order.created
          group: inventory-svc     # a consumer group = competing consumers
          consumer:
            concurrency: 3         # threads, capped by partition count
      kafka:
        binder:
          brokers: localhost:9092
```

The `group` is the important word: every instance of `inventory-svc` joins one group and the partitions are *split among them*, so each message is handled once by the group, not once per instance.

## Partitions, lag and rebalancing

Parallelism has a hard ceiling: **the partition count**. Kafka assigns each partition to exactly one consumer in a group, so with six partitions you can run at most six useful consumers — a seventh sits **idle**, holding a connection and doing nothing.

| Partitions | Consumers in group | Working | Idle |
|-----------|--------------------|---------|------|
| 6 | 3 | 3 (2 partitions each) | 0 |
| 6 | 6 | 6 (1 partition each) | 0 |
| 6 | 9 | 6 | 3 wasted |

**Lag** is the gap between the latest produced offset and the group's committed offset. When the produce rate exceeds the consume rate, lag grows without bound — at 5,000 msg/s produced and 3,000 consumed, you fall 2,000 messages further behind every second, and that backlog *is* your added end-to-end latency. On the bench, push the production rate above what the consumers can drain and watch lag climb, then add consumers up to the partition count and watch it fall.

**Rebalancing** happens whenever a consumer joins or leaves: the group pauses briefly and reassigns partitions. During that stop-the-world window no messages are processed, and any in-flight message not yet committed may be redelivered to its new owner — which is exactly why every consumer must be idempotent (sc15).
