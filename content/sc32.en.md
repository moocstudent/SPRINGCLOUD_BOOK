## Worse than none: the two extremes

CM3 gave you the circuit breaker: three states, and the promise that it stops a slow or failing downstream from taking its callers down with it. What it did not tell you is what to set the numbers to — and a breaker set wrong does not just underperform, it actively hurts you. There are two ways to get it wrong, at opposite ends.

**Too sensitive** is the more insidious. If the breaker trips too easily, it opens during the ordinary noise of a perfectly healthy downstream — a couple of timeouts from a GC pause, a transient blip — and starts rejecting requests that would have succeeded. You have taken a downstream that was fine and, with your own resilience mechanism, manufactured an outage. A too-sensitive breaker is genuinely worse than no breaker: without one, those requests would have gone through.

**Too lax** is the opposite failure and the more common one, because people set the threshold high "to be safe". A breaker that only trips when 90% of calls fail sits closed through the exact cascade it was installed to prevent — threads pile up on the struggling downstream, the caller exhausts its pool, and the breaker opens far too late, if at all. It is a decorative line in a config file that gives you false confidence.

The craft of tuning is finding the middle: trip fast and decisively on real failure, while staying completely blind to transient noise. Every parameter below moves you along that axis.

## Sensitivity: threshold, window, minimum calls

Three parameters together decide how sensitive the breaker is, and the bench shows their combined effect as a curve of trip-probability against the downstream's true failure rate.

`failureRateThreshold` is the obvious one: the failure percentage at which the breaker opens. It sets *where* on the axis the curve flips. 50% is a sane default; 10% invites false trips, 90% removes protection.

`slidingWindowSize` is how many calls (COUNT_BASED) or how much time (TIME_BASED) the breaker averages over. This sets *how steep* the curve is. A tiny window — say 5 calls — is dominated by statistical noise: one bad call is 20%, and the breaker reacts to luck rather than to failure. A large window is steep and noise-proof, but slower to react (it needs a whole window of evidence). This is a real trade: noise-immunity versus detection speed.

`minimumNumberOfCalls` is the guard that stops the breaker from judging on too little evidence. Without it, the first call failing is 1/1 = 100% failure, and the breaker trips on a single unlucky request. Setting it to, say, 10 means the breaker withholds judgement until it has seen enough calls to have an opinion worth trusting.

## Recovery: wait duration and half-open probes

Opening is only half the behaviour; how the breaker recovers matters just as much, and this is where flapping comes from.

`waitDurationInOpenState` is how long the breaker stays open before it tries a probe. Too long and you keep rejecting traffic after the downstream has already recovered — unnecessary downtime. Too short and you probe a downstream that has not had time to heal; the probe fails, the breaker reopens, and you get flapping — open, closed, open, closed — with no steady state. Match it to how long your downstream actually needs to recover.

`permittedNumberOfCallsInHalfOpenState` is how many probe calls the breaker allows before deciding. Too few — one — and a single flaky probe reopens a downstream that had actually recovered, another source of flapping. Enough probes let the breaker make the close/reopen decision on evidence rather than on a coin flip.

## Slow calls are failures too

Here is the parameter people most often miss. A downstream does not have to *error* to hurt you; one that answers every call successfully but takes five seconds to do it still fills your thread pool and cascades, exactly as CM3 warned. A breaker that only counts errors sits closed and useless through this.

Resilience4j lets you count slow calls as failures: `slowCallDurationThreshold` defines what "slow" means (say 2 s), and `slowCallRateThreshold` trips the breaker when too many calls exceed it. Configure this and the breaker protects you from the silent-but-slow failure mode, not just the loud-but-erroring one. The best way to arrive at good numbers is empirical: subscribe to the breaker's state-transition events, watch it under real traffic, and adjust.

## Exercises

1. Set `slidingWindowSize: 5` and `minimumNumberOfCalls: 2`, then send healthy traffic with a 10% random failure rate; watch the breaker false-trip. Widen the window and re-check.
2. Set `failureRateThreshold: 90` and take the downstream to 70% failures; confirm the breaker never opens and the cascade happens.
3. Set `waitDurationInOpenState: 1s` against a downstream that needs ~15s to recover; observe flapping in the transition logs.
4. Add `slowCallDurationThreshold: 2s` / `slowCallRateThreshold: 50`; make the downstream slow-but-not-erroring and confirm the breaker now trips.
