## Why version at all

The whole promise of microservices is that each service ships on its own schedule. That promise has a sharp edge: the order service's API has callers — other services, mobile apps, third parties — and they ship on their own schedules too. In a monolith, when you change a method signature the compiler finds every call site and you fix them all in one commit. Across a network you have no such luxury: you cannot atomically update the caller and the callee, because they are different deployables owned by different teams, sometimes different companies. So the moment you make a change the old caller cannot understand, that caller breaks — in production, at 3 a.m., for someone who never agreed to your release date.

API versioning is how you evolve an interface while its old callers keep working. But before reaching for it, learn its most important lesson: **most changes do not need a version at all.**

## Before you version: backward-compatible evolution

A change is backward-compatible if an old caller, unchanged, still works against the new API. The rules are simple and strict:

- **Add, do not remove or rename.** A new optional field or endpoint is invisible to callers who do not ask for it.
- **Never change a meaning or a type.** Repurposing `status` from a string to an enum, or changing `amount` from cents to dollars, is breaking even if the field name is identical.
- **Callers use a tolerant reader.** A client that ignores fields it does not recognise (rather than rejecting the whole response) survives every additive change automatically.

Follow these and you can evolve for years without ever cutting a v2. The bench above shows it: pick "additive" and the number of broken callers is zero and the versions you maintain stays one. Reach for a real version only when you must make a genuinely breaking change — remove a field, change a type, restructure a resource.

## Where the version goes

When you do need a version, you must decide where the version number lives. There are three common places, and the choice is a trade-off, not a matter of taste:

| Strategy | Example | Pros | Cons |
|---|---|---|---|
| URI path | `GET /v2/orders` | visible, trivial to route at the gateway, cacheable | "version in the URL" offends REST purists |
| Header | `X-API-Version: 2` | clean URLs, one resource identity | invisible, caches must vary on it, hard to curl |
| Media type | `Accept: application/vnd.shop.v2+json` | content negotiation, most "correct" | hardest to test, debug and cache |

In a Spring Cloud system the URI path usually wins for one practical reason: it is trivial for the gateway to route. `/v1/**` goes to order-service-v1, `/v2/**` goes to order-service-v2 — a one-line predicate you already met in the gateway chapter. Header and media-type versioning keep the URL clean but push routing into predicates and force your caches to vary on the version, which is easy to get wrong.

## Running in parallel and sunsetting

A version is a promise you have to keep, and keeping two of them costs. Once you cut v2 you run **both v1 and v2 in parallel** until every caller has moved — and until then you fix bugs, apply security patches, and pay for two code paths. That is why you do not version casually.

The disciplined way to remove the old version is **expand-contract** (also called parallel change), in three steps:

1. **Expand** — ship v2 alongside v1; both are live.
2. **Migrate** — move callers to v2 and watch v1's traffic fall toward zero.
3. **Contract** — after a published sunset date, delete v1.

You communicate the deadline with standard HTTP headers: `Deprecation: true` marks an endpoint as on the way out, and `Sunset: <date>` states when it will be removed, with a `Link` to the successor. Give callers a real window — weeks, not days — and monitor v1's traffic so you retire it on evidence, not hope. The bench makes the cost concrete: a breaking change with parallel versions breaks zero callers but adds weeks of dual maintenance; the same change made in place breaks every caller at once.

## Exercises

1. Take a real endpoint and make a purely additive change (add an optional field). Confirm an old client with a tolerant reader still works, with no version bump.
2. Now make a breaking change and expose it as `/v2`; route `/v1` and `/v2` to two backends with a gateway predicate, and verify both work at once.
3. Add `Deprecation` and `Sunset` headers to your v1 responses; write a tiny client that logs a warning when it sees them.
4. Run the expand-contract cycle end to end on a toy service: ship v2, migrate a client, watch v1 traffic drop, then delete v1.
