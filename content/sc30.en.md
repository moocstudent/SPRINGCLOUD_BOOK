## Why sample at all

In OB1 you gave every request a traceId and drew its waterfall. That is wonderful when you are debugging one request. It is ruinous when you have a million a second. Each request produces a dozen or more spans, each span is a few hundred bytes, and each must be exported over the network, stored and indexed. Multiply it out and tracing everything can cost more than running the services it traces. So at any real scale you **sample**: keep some traces and throw the rest away. The interesting question — the whole subject of this chapter — is which ones you keep, and *when you decide*.

## Head-based sampling: decide at the head, propagate

The simplest scheme is head-based (or "upfront") sampling: at the very first hop, usually the gateway, you make one decision — is this trace sampled? — and propagate that decision to every service the request touches. The propagation is not an afterthought; it is the point. The W3C `traceparent` header carries a flags byte whose last bit is the "sampled" flag, and by default each service is **parent-based**: it honors the bit it received rather than deciding again. That is what keeps a trace consistent — either all of its spans are recorded or none are. A trace with half its spans sampled and half dropped is a broken, unreadable trace, and head sampling avoids that by construction.

Head sampling is cheap (one decision, no buffering) and simple (a probability in config). But it has a hard limitation baked into its name: the decision is made *at the head*, before the request has run, before you know whether it errored or was slow. A 1% head sampler keeps 1% of all traces — and therefore 1% of your errors. The one 3-second checkout a customer is complaining about had a 99% chance of being thrown away before it even finished. The bench makes this visceral: at 1%, of twenty error traces you capture zero.

## Tail-based sampling: decide after the trace completes

Tail-based sampling inverts the timing. Instead of deciding at the start, every service records its spans and ships them to a collector, and the collector holds all of a trace's spans in a buffer until the trace completes. Only then, knowing the full outcome, does it decide whether to keep the trace. Now you can express the policy you actually wanted: keep 100% of traces that contain an error, keep 100% of traces slower than 800 ms, and keep 1% of the boring rest. You never miss the incident, because you decide *after* the incident has happened.

The power comes at a real infrastructural price. Something must buffer every in-flight span until its trace finishes — that is memory, and a `decision_wait` you must tune (too short and you decide before slow traces finish). And there is a routing constraint that trips people up: all spans of one trace must reach the **same collector instance**, or no single collector ever sees the whole trace to judge it — so you put a trace-ID-aware load balancer in front of your collector fleet. This is why tail sampling is an OpenTelemetry Collector feature, not a one-line Spring property.

## Trade-offs

Neither is universally right; they sit at different points of a cost / visibility / infrastructure triangle:

| | Head-based | Tail-based |
|---|---|---|
| Decision made | at the start, before the outcome | at the end, knowing the outcome |
| Catches every error | no (only the sampled fraction) | yes |
| Cost / complexity | cheap, one config line | a buffering collector, tuned |
| Consistency | via the propagated sampled bit | everyone records, collector decides |
| Where it lives | the app (Micrometer) | the Collector |

A common, pragmatic answer is to combine them: a modest head sample to bound the raw volume, plus tail sampling in the collector to guarantee that whatever survives, the errors and the slow traces are always among it. Start with head sampling because it is free; reach for tail sampling the first time you go to investigate an incident and find its trace was never kept.

## Exercises

1. Set `management.tracing.sampling.probability` to 0.01 and generate an error; confirm you usually cannot find its trace. Raise it to 1.0 and watch your storage bill (and the trace appear).
2. Stand up an OpenTelemetry Collector with a `tail_sampling` processor that keeps all errors; reproduce the error and confirm its trace is always retained at a low base rate.
3. Inspect the `traceparent` header at two services in one trace and confirm the sampled bit is identical — the propagated, consistent decision.
4. Break tail sampling on purpose: run two collector instances without trace-ID routing, and observe traces arriving split across them so no policy sees the whole trace.
