## Two questions, two tools

Tracing answers "why was this one request slow." Metrics answer a different question: "is the system healthy right now, and which way is the trend heading." One is a magnifying glass on a single request; the other is a dashboard over millions. You need both, and this chapter is about the dashboard — how to build it, and the one statistical habit that decides whether it tells you the truth.

### RED: three numbers per service

For a request-serving service, three signals catch almost everything that matters. **Rate** — requests per second. **Errors** — the fraction that failed (5xx, exceptions, timeouts). **Duration** — the distribution of how long requests took. That is the RED method, and its virtue is that it is small enough to actually watch. (Its sibling USE — utilisation, saturation, errors — is for resources like CPU and disk; RED is for request flows.)

| Signal | Question it answers | Watch for |
|---|---|---|
| Rate | how much traffic | a sudden drop (upstream broke) or spike (retry storm) |
| Errors | how much is failing | the rate crossing the SLO, not just "> 0" |
| Duration | how slow | the tail (P99), never the average |

### Why the average lies

Duration is where teams hurt themselves, because the instinctive summary — the average — is the one number that hides the problem. Take a realistic mix: 99% of requests finish in 20 ms, and 1% hit a slow path at 2 s. The average is 0.99 × 20 + 0.01 × 2000 ≈ 40 ms. Forty milliseconds looks wonderful on a dashboard. But one user in a hundred waited two full seconds — that is your P99 experience, and it is dreadful. The average took the pain of the unlucky 1% and smeared it across the happy 99% until it disappeared.

It gets worse under load, because latency is a queue, and queues do not degrade linearly. As a service approaches saturation the mean can barely move while the tail detonates:

| Load | Mean | P95 | P99 | P999 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1,000 req/s (~30% util) | 22 ms | 35 ms | 60 ms | 180 ms |
| 4,000 req/s (~70% util) | 26 ms | 55 ms | 210 ms | 900 ms |
| 5,500 req/s (~95% util) | 40 ms | 140 ms | 1,400 ms | 6 s |

The mean crept from 22 to 40 ms — a dashboard watching the average would call this fine. Meanwhile P99 went from 60 ms to 1.4 s, and one request in a thousand is now waiting six seconds. This is why the tail percentiles — P95/P99/P999 — decide user experience, and the bench lets you turn the rate up and watch the mean and the P99 part company exactly like this.

### The stack: Actuator → Prometheus → Grafana

Spring Boot Actuator exposes the numbers, Micrometer is the vendor-neutral facade that records them, Prometheus scrapes `/actuator/prometheus` every 15 s, and Grafana draws it. One caveat drives the config: **percentiles cannot be averaged across instances**. If each pod pre-computes its own P99 and ships that number, there is no correct way to combine ten pods' P99s into a fleet P99. So you ship *histograms* — bucket counts — and let Prometheus compute the quantile at query time with `histogram_quantile()`.

```yaml
management:
  endpoints:
    web:
      exposure:
        include: health,info,prometheus     # scrape target
  metrics:
    distribution:
      percentiles-histogram:
        http.server.requests: true          # emit histogram buckets
      slo:
        http.server.requests: 300ms         # add a bucket at the SLO
```

With buckets in place, `histogram_quantile(0.99, sum(rate(http_server_requests_seconds_bucket[5m])) by (le))` gives a correct fleet-wide P99 that a per-instance average never could.

### SLOs turn reliability into a budget

A service-level objective is a promise with a number: "99% of requests faster than 300 ms, measured over 30 days." Its power is the **error budget** — the 1% you are allowed to miss. At a 99.9% availability target, that budget is about 43 minutes a month; at 99.99%, about 4 minutes. This makes reliability spendable: if this month's budget is untouched, ship features faster and take risks; if a bad deploy burned it in one afternoon, freeze releases and fix stability. The budget ends the sterile argument between "move fast" and "be reliable" by pricing it — and it tells you when a P99 regression is a genuine emergency versus a number you can afford to sit on for a week.

## Exercises

1. Expose `/actuator/prometheus`, run Prometheus (`prom/prometheus`) scraping it and Grafana on top, and put `http_server_requests_seconds` on a graph. Fire mixed traffic and confirm the endpoint updates every scrape.
2. Turn on `percentiles-histogram` for `http.server.requests`, then in Prometheus compute `histogram_quantile(0.99, sum(rate(http_server_requests_seconds_bucket[5m])) by (le))`. Compare that fleet P99 against each instance's mean.
3. Write an SLO for one endpoint ("99% < 300 ms over 30 days"), compute its monthly error budget in minutes, and build a Grafana panel that shows how much of the budget is burned.
4. In the bench, raise the request rate step by step and record the mean and P99 at each step. Find the utilisation at which P99 crosses a 300 ms SLO while the mean is still under 50 ms — and note how invisible that is on an average-only dashboard.
